Benchmarks

Five clients, eight scenarios, identical hardware and providers, runs interleaved so provider drift cancels. The metric is time to a usable file: downloaded, verified, extracted. Includes the legs we don't win.

Methodology first

The setup

  • Clients: nzbfast · SABnzbd 5.0.4 · NZBGet 26.2 · rustnzb 1.3.4 · Weaver (the two closest Rust peers), all on the same machine per scenario (Apple M-series boxes: "Europe" M1 Ultra on 10 GbE, "East Coast" M3 Ultra ~2.4–3.0 Gbps line). Weaver appears in the reproducible client-ceiling leg below; the provider legs predate it.
  • Fairness: same 6 providers × 8 connections, TLS everywhere, legs run back-to-back interleaved. Every competitor tuned to its documented best: SABnzbd got pipelining_requests=8 (it ships 1, i.e. unpipelined; that setting alone took its 190 GB time from 24m24s to 19m02s), NZBGet got ArticleCache/DirectWrite/DirectUnpack/ParQuick, rustnzb its documented config.
  • End state: a verified, extracted, usable file, not "download finished". That's what you actually wait for.
  • Reproducibility: every claim traces to a dated entry in our benchmark log, recorded with the driver scripts that produced it: wall-clock + interface byte counters + process-tree peak RSS, 1–2 s resolution.

Three shapes of the same problem: nzbfast's tail-overlap keeps the line busy edge-to-edge; NZBGet never idles but runs ~35% slower while unpacking concurrently; SABnzbd downloads fast, then leaves the line dark 61% of the time during serial post-processing. In the resilience variant (one job with encrypted RAR headers), SABnzbd's queue wedged on the encrypted job and only 1 of 3 jobs ever completed; nzbfast parked it with a clear message and finished the rest.

The honest column

The legs we don't win

Damaged store-mode post

20 poisoned articles in a 7.4 GB REMUX: NZBGet finishes usable in 53 s, nzbfast in 58 s. Our repair completes in 32 s, re-extracts all 62 volumes, and the output is byte-exact, and it pulls a near-theoretical-minimum 22 recovery blocks for the 20 needed, against NZBGet's full PAR2 pass. But NZBGet takes the leg by five seconds, so here it is.

Par-only reconstruction

The poster deleted every data article and left only a 100%-redundancy PAR2 set. All four clients rebuild the whole volume set from parity; NZBGet does it fastest, 8 s to our 19 s on a 1.5 GB post (rustnzb 49 s, SABnzbd 6m 30s). Rebuilding an entire set from nothing is the one repair shape where an in-stream engine has no edge.

Why show a loss at all? Because the wins are only believable next to it. Every number on this page comes from the same interleaved runs, and the repo keeps the whole thing as a full lab notebook, negative results included.

The same four jobs, re-run at hard memory budgets of 2 GB, 1 GB and 256 MB: what the auto-sizer would pick on an 8 GB box, a 4 GB box, and a 2 GB NAS. Every leg produced a correct, fully verified, extracted file; peak RSS tracked the budget, not the job. Time to usable file, 10 GbE line:

The 20–40% premium on the big jobs is a 10 GbE artifact: spilled blocks only cost time when the line outruns the disk. The same 87 GB job at the same budgets on a ~2.4 Gbps line measured −1% to +7%, noise. On a typical home connection a small budget is close to free at any job size. A NAS profile (2 connections, 256 MB budget) finished the 35 GB job in 0.4 GB peak RSS. A 2 GB NAS can run this. No other client offers a memory ceiling at all.

Scenario 7 · archives inside archives

Ten nested shapes: who finishes without you

Posts increasingly arrive nested: a RAR inside a RAR, a 7z tucked into a store archive, a ladder of layers, a password chain. This round grades what's left for the operator. auto means every payload extracted, byte-identical, no hands; manual means the client reported success but left an inner archive sitting in the output directory for you to open yourself.

auto-complete, all ten shapes8/106/102/102/10

Loopback rig, machine-generated corpus, all four clients on the same machine, graded by content hash, so a client that renames the payload still gets credit. Because inner layers are denested on the fly, nzbfast holds one ~1.5 GB copy on disk where write-out-and-unpack clients hold ~3 GB, and finishes these legs in 1–2 s against 4–8 s. On the password chain, each layer's password ships in a file the layer above it extracts: nzbfast reads it and unlocks all three levels; the others stop and wait for you to type it. The two shapes it doesn't auto-complete are graded harshly on purpose: a 10-level ladder past its default depth cap and a post damaged at all three levels. On both it recovers more payloads than any other client, but exits nonzero instead of calling a partial job a success, so both count as failures here.

Scenario 8 · five clients, reproducible content

Client ceiling: 9.68 GB served from RAM, Weaver included

Every leg above runs against commercial providers, so you have to take our word for the line conditions. This one you can rebuild yourself: the content is Blender's open movies (Tears of Steel 4K, Sintel, Big Buck Bunny 4K at 60 and 30 fps), CC-BY 3.0, packed store-mode RAR plus 10% PAR2 into 53 files and 14,521 articles, served over loopback NNTP from RAM by a rig in this repository. No provider is involved, so this measures the client itself, with provider variance removed rather than averaged out.

Because the articles come from memory, the absolute times are far shorter than any real download and are not comparable to the provider legs above. Read the ratios and the disk column, not the seconds.

Time to finish runs from starting the job to all four videos being on disk, verified and extracted. Peak disk is the most disk the client ever occupied at any instant, sampled twice a second, and it is the column that matters most here: one-pass needs about 1× the content, everything else about 2×. All five ended holding the same 8.6 GB once they had tidied up, which is exactly why the peak is the interesting figure. Every client produced all four payloads sha256-identical to source. Each is shown at whichever connection count made it fastest (8, 16 and 32 were tried on each; nzbfast was flat at all three, so more connections would not have helped it).

Every competitor at its documented best, and what that cost us. A rival must never lose because we misconfigured it, and auditing this round turned up four defects that had all flattered nzbfast. SABnzbd was running unpipelined: it rewrites its own ini at startup and silently reverted pipelining_requests=8 to the shipped 1, so it has to be set over the API afterwards and read back to confirm. Fixing that alone took it from 57 s to 14 s, a 4× gain. NZBGet was running with its article cache off, because a standalone -c config inherits NZBGet's built-in defaults rather than the values in its shipped conf: 28 s to 19 s. Weaver needs warming, since it auto-ramps to 120 connections and tunes its pools on the first job, and its download CLI never exits, so it is measured as a persistent daemon with a discarded warm-up pass. Cold it looks broken; warmed it is the closest competitor here. Weaver's 8 s is therefore a warmed daemon at 120 connections against our cold one-shot at 8. That is each client at its best by our own rule, but the asymmetry is real and we would rather state it than bury it.

Weaver also looked one-pass at first: a single measurement after its run showed it holding about the same 8.6 GB as everyone else. That was the tidied aftermath, since it deletes volumes once extraction finishes. Polled every half second its true peak is 17.9 GB, the highest of the five. For anything that self-cleans, report the peak, not the leftovers.

Component shootouts

The unpack and repair engines, raced solo

Extraction and PAR2 are our own native code, so we also race them standalone against the field on identical corpora. A time only counts when the output is byte-identical to the source payload.

RAR extraction · 8 archive shapes

Against unrar 7.23, 7-Zip, bsdtar, unar and the upstream rars crate on an Apple M3 Ultra, nzbfast's extractor wins or ties every shape: 400 small files 0.13 s to unrar's 0.66 s, solid 0.50 vs 0.87, encrypted 0.49 vs 0.82, a RAR7 128 MB-dictionary archive 0.71 vs 0.92. Re-run on a 20-core M1 Ultra and on a 14-core Intel laptop, the result holds on every shape, and on the laptop the margins widen: the parallel decode paths scale into the extra threads. Every reported time produced sha256-identical output.

PAR2 verify + repair · 1 GiB set

Against classic par2cmdline, the SIMD par2cmdline-turbo fork and MultiPar, nzbfast has the fastest verify and the fastest repair on every machine benched. 20-core desktop: clean verify 0.40 s to turbo's 1.08 and classic's 3.67; repairing 101 damaged blocks 1.26 s vs 2.61 and 7.52. 14-core laptop: verify 1.26 vs 1.48, repair 2.57 vs 3.62. Every repaired file byte-identical. One honest note: we don't create PAR2 (a downloader doesn't need to, and ParPar owns that leg).

Transport proof

The engine saturates real lines

  • 8.31 Gbps aggregate on a measured-8.2 Gbps 10 GbE line (Europe M1 Ultra, 4 providers, 14,000 articles, 0 errors, 0 reconnects).
  • 9.16–9.27 Gbps steady transport ticks on the same line, ≈98% of practical TCP goodput.
  • 5.44 Gbps sustained through a full 20.4 GB job including in-stream verification.
  • Pipelining replicated at every RTT: +64% at 63 ms Starlink, +170–177% at 14 ms datacenter, +270% at 31 ms East Coast. It defeats TCP congestion-window decay, not just latency.
  • Backpressure proof: cap the write stage 4× below the network and input locks to the cap ±1% within 2 s, RAM falls, zero swaps. A slow disk can never balloon memory.
  • Compute headroom: measured decode+verify ceilings of 86–165 Gbps on Apple silicon. The network is the bottleneck by 4–50× on every machine tested.
Standing rule from the repo: every perf claim cites a reproducible command and the conditions it ran under. The whole thing lives in the repo as a dated lab notebook, negative results and wrong turns included.
