Methodology first

The setup

  • Clients: nzbfast · SABnzbd 5.0.4 · NZBGet 26.2 · rustnzb 1.3.4 · Weaver, all on the same machine per scenario (Apple M-series boxes: "Europe" M1 Ultra on 10 GbE, "East Coast" M3 Ultra ~2.4–3.0 Gbps line). The provider legs below predate Weaver's inclusion; it appears in the reproducible client-ceiling leg. nzbfast's own figures span builds 1.0.2 to 1.0.8 as the engine changed; older legs understate the shipping build rather than flatter it.
  • Fairness: same 6 providers × 8 connections, TLS everywhere, legs run back-to-back interleaved. Every competitor tuned to its documented best: SABnzbd got pipelining_requests=8 (it ships 1, i.e. unpipelined; that setting alone took its 190 GB time from 24m24s to 19m02s), NZBGet got ArticleCache/DirectWrite/DirectUnpack/ParQuick, rustnzb its documented config.
  • End state: a verified, extracted, usable file, not "download finished". That's what you actually wait for.
  • Reproducibility: every claim is dated and measured the same way: elapsed time + interface byte counters + process-tree peak RSS, 1–2 s resolution. Where a leg runs on a publicly posted NZB, we name it so you can run it yourself.

The census

Two shapes are 98.6% of the data

We looked at 890,852 releases, 1.6 million files and 79.6 TB across the two busiest movie and TV groups, then fetched and read the archive headers of a thousand real posts to confirm what the filenames only implied. Counted by bytes rather than by post, because a million tiny files matter less than one large one.

  • 2.5% of releases are multi-volume RAR archives, and they are 84% of all the data. Everything else is a long tail of small files.
  • Inside that: 84% stored (packed with no compression) and 14% encrypted. Together, 98.6%.
  • Compressed archives are 1.4%. Solid archives, the slowest kind to unpack, turned out to be extinct: not one in roughly 700 archives we read.

That reshaped what we work on. There is little point tuning a compression path that carries 1.4% of the data, so we tuned the two that carry the rest.

Encryption is not spread evenly either. It scales with size:

Release sizeShare of all dataStoredEncrypted
1-5 GB29%94%2%
5-20 GB39%97%2%
20-60 GB20%67%33%

Ordinary downloads are almost always plain stored archives. The big ones are a coin flip between stored and encrypted. Below is how nzbfast handles each.

The honest column

The legs we don't win

Both legs that used to sit here have been re-measured on the shipping build and neither is a loss any more: the damaged store-mode post now takes us 30 s against NZBGet's 38 s, and par-only reconstruction now completes in 9 s where it took 19 s, on a leg where NZBGet returns in the same time but delivers nothing. We are leaving the section here rather than deleting it. This is where our losses go, and the next round that finds one will put it back.

Why show a loss at all? Because the wins are only believable next to it. Every number on this page comes from the same interleaved runs, and a leg we lose stays published until a re-run replaces it.

Scenario 7 · archives inside archives

Ten nested shapes: who finishes without you

Posts increasingly arrive nested: a RAR inside a RAR, a 7z tucked into a store archive, a ladder of layers, a password chain. This round grades what's left for the operator. auto means every payload extracted, byte-identical, no hands; manual means the client reported success but left an inner archive sitting in the output directory for you to open yourself.

Loopback rig, machine-generated corpus, all four clients on the same machine, graded by content hash, so a client that renames the payload still gets credit. Because inner layers are denested on the fly, nzbfast holds one ~1.5 GB copy on disk where write-out-and-unpack clients hold ~3 GB, and finishes these legs in 1–2 s against 4–8 s. On the password chain, each layer's password ships in a file the layer above it extracts: nzbfast reads it and unlocks all three levels; the others stop and wait for you to type it. The two shapes it doesn't auto-complete are graded harshly on purpose: a 10-level ladder past its default depth cap and a post damaged at all three levels. On both it recovers more payloads than any other client, but exits nonzero instead of calling a partial job a success, so both count as failures here.

Every competitor at its documented best. A rival must never lose because we misconfigured it, so each client's settings are published with the round. Two are easy to get wrong and worth knowing if you run your own: SABnzbd rewrites its own ini at startup and reverts pipelining_requests to the shipped 1, so pipelining has to be set over the API afterwards and read back to confirm; and a standalone NZBGet -c config inherits NZBGet's built-in defaults rather than the values in its shipped conf, which silently leaves the article cache off. Both ran with those corrected. Weaver is measured warm, because it auto-ramps its connection count and tunes its pools on the first job and its download CLI never exits, so it runs as a persistent daemon with a discarded warm-up pass. Its 8 s is therefore a warmed daemon at 120 connections against our cold one-shot at 8. That is each client at its best by our own rule, but the asymmetry is real and we would rather state it than bury it.

Peak disk is polled twice a second rather than read once at the end, which matters more than it sounds: every client here deletes its volumes after extraction, so all five finish holding the same 8.6 GB and four of them look one-pass if you only measure the aftermath. Polled through the run, four peak near 18 GB. For anything that self-cleans, report the peak, not the leftovers.

Shape one · 84% of the data

Plain stored archives: a queue of jobs

Three real releases, queued one after another so each waits for the previous to finish. That is how a queue actually drains, and it is where the cost of starting each job shows up. Six providers, the same connection count for every client, same machine and network.

nzbfast7.05 s
NZBGet 26.210.66 s · 1.51x slower
SABnzbd 5.0.417.96 s · 2.55x slower

nzbfast is 1.51x faster than NZBGet and 2.55x faster than SABnzbd on the single most common shape on usenet.

Every client was checked at 8, 16 and 32 connections per server and is shown at its own best, because a competitor should never lose for being badly configured. Worth knowing if you tune your own setup: NZBGet got about twice as slow at 32 connections, and SABnzbd behaved the same way in our earlier testing. More connections is not more speed. nzbfast was flat across the whole range, so the setting will not catch you out.

We also check that each client actually produced the files, rather than trusting it when it reports success. One client in our testing reported three completed downloads in about a second each and had written nothing at all.

Shape two · 14% of the data, half of everything over 60 GB

Encrypted archives, and half the writing

On a real 94 GB encrypted release, nzbfast now writes 90 GB to your disk where it used to write 180 GB, and finishes the moment the download does instead of pausing for a separate unlock pass.

Encrypted archives were the last shape we could not handle in a single pass. nzbfast had to write the locked data to disk, read it all back, unlock it, and write it out again. Twice the writing, twice the disk, and a wait at the end that grew with the release. It now unlocks each piece as it arrives, so the locked data never reaches the disk at all.

The most disk used at once is now 89.6 GB, which is exactly the size of the file you asked for. There is no moment during an encrypted download when nzbfast needs room for a second copy.

The shape of it

Disk in use during one download, sampled every five seconds. The two lines are identical right up to the moment the download finishes. Then the old one climbs to 166 GB: that is the unlock pass, writing the finished file while the locked copy is still on disk. The new line simply stops, because the file it was writing was already unlocked.

Why it matters

Your drive does half the work

Flash storage wears out by being written to. Downloading a 94 GB release used to cost your drive 180 GB of writing; now it costs 90 GB. On a NAS with hard disks the same change removes a long single-threaded pass at the end of every encrypted download, which on a slower machine takes minutes rather than the 20 seconds we measured here.

What we are not claiming

  • The 20 second pause we measured is the best case for the old behaviour. It was measured on a fast 32-core workstation with hardware acceleration for the unlocking. On a low-power NAS, which is where most people run this, the old pass is far slower and the improvement correspondingly larger. We quote the smaller number because it is the one we measured.
  • The 5.6% faster finish is real but modest, and it is entirely the removed pause at the end. The download itself is not faster. In our runs the new behaviour's download leg was in fact marginally slower than the old one, which is why we are confident the 5.6% is not measurement drift flattering us.

Light damage, the common field case. A handful of failed articles is far more typical than 101 dead blocks, so this round adds a 3-block repair leg: 0.58 s against par2-turbo's 1.12 s on 32 cores, 0.69 s against 1.37 s on 20 cores. It is worth saying what did not move. The 1.0.8 repair rewrite spreads the fold across rows and columns, and rows are the missing-block count, so light damage is exactly where the old code left a big machine idle. In the fold microbenchmark alone that is a 3.65× speedup. End to end it is worth almost nothing, because the fold is about a millisecond of a run dominated by reading and MD5-ing a gigabyte. Both halves are true and the second one is the one that reaches users.

RAR recovery records: repairing without PAR2

When PAR2 cannot cover the damage, the recovery record inside the RAR itself is the last line of defence. Until 1.0.8 ours failed on any archive over about 13 MB, so this leg could not be run at all. Damage is three 3,000-byte holes at 20%, 50% and 80% through the protected region. Both tools produced output byte-identical to the pristine file, and ours is byte-identical to what rar r itself writes.

We lead to about 128 MB and RARLab pulls ahead by 512 MB, so the crossover sits somewhere in the low hundreds of MB. That last row is a loss and it stays on the page: our repair streams rather than holding whole volumes in memory, which costs throughput at the top end and buys a bounded working set. On the 8 GB to 20 GB volumes a daemon actually meets, holding the volume is not an option, so it is the right trade even where it loses. Measured against the buffered path on the same archive: 1.4× slower to run, 2.3× less peak memory.

What else moved in 1.0.8, and where it does not show. Three more engine changes landed that these corpora cannot see, listed here so the numbers above are not read as the whole story: rebuilding a .rev volume now does one erasure solve instead of one per byte; RAR5 archives with tens of thousands of members resolve each member once rather than walking the list per worker (3× less processor time at 40,000 members); and the RAR1.3 bit reader works a word at a time (2×). None of them appear above, because the shapes here have 400 members, no .rev set, and no RAR1.3.

What we deliberately do not do: we never create PAR2. A downloader has no reason to, and ParPar owns that leg. We also buy speed with memory on both engines: extraction peaks around 240 MB against unrar's 41 MB, and verify around 126 MB against turbo's 7 MB, because these are the inline engines that ride a live download rather than standalone one-shots. If you want the smallest possible resident set for a standalone job, the dedicated tools still win that column.

Every number on this page is a dated run with its exact command and conditions recorded, negative results and abandoned approaches included. These pages are the published record, and they get fleshed out as further rounds land.

Transport proof

The engine saturates real lines

  • 8.31 Gbps aggregate on a measured-8.2 Gbps 10 GbE line (Europe M1 Ultra, 4 providers, 14,000 articles, 0 errors, 0 reconnects).
  • 9.16–9.27 Gbps steady transport ticks on the same line, ≈98% of practical TCP goodput.
  • 5.44 Gbps sustained through a full 20.4 GB job including in-stream verification.
  • Pipelining replicated at every RTT: +64% at 63 ms Starlink, +170–177% at 14 ms datacenter, +270% at 31 ms East Coast. It defeats TCP congestion-window decay, not just latency.
  • Backpressure proof: cap the write stage 4× below the network and input locks to the cap ±1% within 2 s, RAM falls, zero swaps. A slow disk can never balloon memory.
  • Compute headroom: measured decode+verify ceilings of 86–165 Gbps on Apple silicon. The network is the bottleneck by 4–50× on every machine tested.

Re-measured 31 July 2026 on the current build, on a local rig that serves articles from RAM so only the client is being timed: downloading costs 0.64 CPU-seconds per GB on the East Coast M3 Ultra and 0.74 on the Europe M1 Ultra, rising to 0.83 and 1.06 with TLS on - and the pipeline still runs at the operating system's own ~32 Gbps local-socket limit, TLS included. On a 2022 Intel laptop (i7-1280P, Windows 11) the client's own work is about the same 0.7 CPU-seconds per GB; the Windows kernel and antivirus add roughly 1.6 more on top, and that platform's local-socket path tops out near 8 Gbps.
